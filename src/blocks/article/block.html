Pricing studio work without guessing

Five habits from studios that stopped losing money on fixed-fee projects, and the numbers they check before every quote.

ID Inès Duarte Managing partner, Foundry & Field · 22 October 2026

On this page

  1. Start from capacity
  2. Price the outcome
  3. Scope as decisions
  4. Bill in milestones
  5. Review every project

Start from capacity, not hourly rates

An hourly rate tells you what an hour is worth; it says nothing about how many of those hours you can actually sell. A team of six rarely bills more than 65% of its time once meetings, pitches and admin are counted.

Before quoting, look at the next eight weeks: who is free, who is already at 90%, and which week the project would really start. A good price for a project you cannot staff is still a bad quote.

Price the outcome, then check the hours

Clients buy a launch, a rebrand or a new menu, not 140 hours. Anchor the conversation on what changes for them, then estimate the hours as a private sanity check, never as the headline.

Rule of thumb: if the outcome price is less than 1.4 times your estimated cost, the project needs a smaller scope, not a smaller margin.

Write the scope as a list of decisions

Deliverables invite endless rounds. Decisions close them. Instead of “logo, colours, typography”, list what the client will sign off:

  • One primary mark, chosen from three routes.
  • A palette of five colours, tested on uncoated paper.
  • Two typefaces, licensed for web and print.

Anything that reopens a signed decision is a change request, priced before it starts.

Bill in milestones clients can approve

Tie each invoice to a decision the client has already approved: 30% at kick-off, 40% when the route is chosen, 30% at hand-off. Payment stops being a negotiation because the work it pays for is visibly done.

Studios that moved to approval-based milestones in Sable cut their average time to payment from twelve days to four.

Review every project after delivery

Thirty minutes is enough. Compare the quoted hours with the logged ones, note which decision took longest, and adjust the next quote of the same kind. Over a year, those small corrections are worth more than any rate increase.

Keep the review in the project itself, next to the brief, so the next person quoting similar work finds it.
